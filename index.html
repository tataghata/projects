Audit a term

Each card puts three references side by side: the law, what the body knows, and how the word is used now. Then what survives the transfer, what doesn't, what gets smuggled in, and whether a person who never studied physics could justify the load-bearing entailment from something they have felt. Underline style is the verdict.

Corpus

All audited cards, filterable. Sourcing is where a lay speaker's knowledge of the source term comes from: the body (push, fall, heavy), the craft (tools and machines people have used or watched), or the textbook (things only ever learned formally).

Findings

The hypothesis this corpus was built to test: terms that entered language from the body should be backable almost by default; terms that entered from the textbook should fail almost by construction, because nobody can back them from experience, so they are borrowing authority. Craft terms should sit between. Both charts are computed from the cards on this page; change a verdict in the data and they change.

Two things the second chart shows that the first can't. A textbook term can transfer faithfully and still be unbackable: critical mass carries the reproduction-number logic marketplaces need, and no speaker can feel it. And a body term can be perfectly backable and still wrong about its target: blow off steam is exact for boilers and the evidence on venting anger points the other way. Backable, structurally faithful, and true of the target are three different properties, and a metaphor can have any combination.

    Queue

    Terms collected but not yet audited. Each is tagged with a source domain and a sourcing guess; both are provisional and some will move. The sentence check marks these with a dotted line so you know the word has been noticed but not judged. This is where the hypothesis gets tested.


    Method

    "Does the metaphor hold?" is three questions that give different answers, so every card answers all three.

    Against the law
    What the physics actually says, including the properties people forget: conservation, symmetry, thresholds, what the idealization drops. Almost every business metaphor fails somewhere here; nobody's momentum conserves. That alone isn't a verdict.
    Against the body
    What a person who never studied physics knows from living: heavy things are hard to move, moving things slow down, a tight shoe hurts, a lid holds and then gives. This is the standard the site cares about. A metaphor is backable when the entailment it is being used to carry corresponds to something the speaker has felt or watched, and that entailment transfers to the target. It is partly backable when some common entailments are felt but a load-bearing one is an idealization or exists only in the textbook. It is not backable when the load-bearing entailment can only be known formally, or when the usage inverts the physics.
    Against the target
    Whether the domain the word is applied to actually has the structure the usage relies on. This is empirical and sometimes exactly what is in dispute, so the site mostly exposes the entailment rather than ruling on it: if this metaphor holds, such-and-such must be true of your domain. Is it?

    Two further ratings are kept separate from backability, because they measure different things. Structure (faithful, partial, poor) asks whether the target has the relational shape the usage needs, regardless of whether anyone can feel it. Load (high, medium, low) estimates how much argumentative weight the word usually carries: dead metaphors score low, and auditing them is pedantry.

    Each failure is classed by type, so that the corpus can say where transfers tend to break rather than only that they do:

    idealization
    borrows a frictionless, lossless, closed-system, or one-sided version of the physics — momentum that never slows, grind that wears only the work.
    conservation
    treats something as a conserved substance that can be poured and must go somewhere — liquidity, pipeline contents, anger as steam.
    threshold
    borrows a sharp limit or irreversibility the target lacks — escape velocity, a slope with no footholds, a tipping point with nothing stored.
    agency
    imports passivity or deliberateness the target lacks — the harness that in practice holds the reins, the anchor nobody chose.
    inversion
    the usage means roughly the opposite of the physics — quantum leap, inflection point.
    sourcing
    the load-bearing entailment exists only in the textbook; no speaker can back it — bandwidth, entropy, exponential.
    structure
    the target lacks the relational structure the usage relies on — a flywheel asked to be an engine, diffusion that actually needs contact.
    target
    the physics transfers and the felt experience is real; the target domain just doesn't behave that way — blow off steam.

    Sourcing records where a lay speaker's knowledge of the source comes from, not where the entailment comes from. Gravity is body-sourced (everyone feels weight) even though data gravity's load-bearing entailments are textbook-only; that gap is what the failure type records.

    The sentence check is string matching against the audited terms, their aliases, and the queue, with common inflections. It cannot tell a literal pipeline from a metaphorical one, and it cannot find a metaphor expressed in words that aren't in the corpus. Its job is to make you pause on the word, not to grade the sentence.

    Usage. Where a card says current usage was verified, two or more live sources were read in October 2026 and paraphrased. Everywhere else, the usages are characteristic phrasings from the author's knowledge and the card says so. Verifying the rest is the first item of future work.

    What a verdict is for. The purpose is not to stop people using physics words. It is the opposite: to let someone who has never opened a physics book use "momentum" and mean it, because they can point at the cart. The question every card ends with is the one to carry into your own sentences.

    The case against this artifact

    Written by the author, against the author. Read it before trusting the verdicts.

    • It is itself an authority stamp. A page that says "backable" in green is doing the thing it warns about: lending physics prestige to language. The mitigations are that there is no score, every verdict shows its reasons, and the final word on each card is a question for the speaker, not a ruling. Whether that is enough is for the reader to judge.
    • One author, one judgment. The verdicts are the opinion of a language model working with one person, not a corpus study, not a panel, not a survey of speakers. Several are contestable on the page: whether balance's zero-sum reading is really the common one; whether catalyst used as "cause" is loose or just lexicalized; whether trickle-down deserves a verdict at all given that nobody who holds the view uses the word.
    • Circularity. The same author selected the corpus and assigned the verdicts, with the body / craft / textbook hypothesis already in mind. The findings charts therefore describe a set of judgments, not an experiment. The queue was built with the same mind. A real test needs terms chosen by someone else and verdicts assigned blind to sourcing.
    • "The body knows" is a population claim. A driller's body knows torque; a child's does not; a sailor's knows anchors and a programmer's knows cold starts. Experiential physics varies by trade, culture, and era, and this page writes as if there were one body. The sourcing tags are guesses about a typical professional speaker of English in 2026.
    • The sourcing split is fuzzy. Pressure is body and textbook at once; gravity is felt daily and understood only formally; harness is craft for some and lexicalized jargon for most. Several tags could be argued either way, and moving a handful would change the charts.
    • Usage was mostly not searched. Fifty-five of fifty-seven cards describe characteristic usage from memory. Two were checked against live sources, and in both cases the search changed the card — which suggests the other fifty-five would change too.
    • Dead metaphors. Nobody saying "bandwidth" is making a claim about frequency ranges, and auditing them is pedantry. The load rating tries to mark these, but the line between a dead metaphor and a live one that has merely stopped being noticed is exactly the line this page is bad at seeing.
    • Backable is not true. The site's own blow-off-steam card shows a metaphor that is fully experiential, structurally exact, and wrong about its target. Passing the body test does not make a claim correct, and a reader who takes green underlines as endorsements has been misled by the design.
    • Replacements can be worse. "Self-reinforcing loop" is more accurate than "flywheel" and nobody will say it. Some of the suggested phrasings trade a wrong vivid word for a right dull one, and in persuasion that is not a free trade. Sometimes the imperfect metaphor is the best available compression and the honest move is to use it and name its boundary.
    • The matcher is naive. It flags literal uses, misses paraphrases, and cannot see a physics metaphor built from words outside the corpus. It will mark "the pipeline from Cushing" and miss "things are picking up speed on their own," which is momentum in plain clothes.
    • Scope. The target was five hundred audited terms; there are fifty-seven, with the rest queued and tagged provisionally. The corpus leans toward business and technology speech, because that is the author's working world, and thin on the law, medicine, and sport, which borrow physics just as freely.
